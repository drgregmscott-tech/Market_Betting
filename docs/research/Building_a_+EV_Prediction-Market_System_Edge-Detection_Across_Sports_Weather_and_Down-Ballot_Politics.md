# Building a +EV Prediction-Market Analysis System: Proven Probability-Modeling and Edge-Detection Techniques Across Sports, Climate/Commodities, and Down-Ballot Politics

## TL;DR
- **The single most durable, well-documented edge across all three domains is the favorite-longshot bias and its prediction-market analogue "political underconfidence"** — on Kalshi, Bürgi, Deng & Whelan's study of 300,000+ contracts found investors who buy contracts costing less than 10¢ lose over 60% of their money, while contracts priced above 50¢ earn a small positive return; the practical system rule is to systematically buy favorites (and "No" on longshots) rather than chase cheap tickets.
- **Domain by domain, the exploitable edge is largest where sophisticated attention is thinnest**: same-day weather (beat retail with free NOAA/METAR/GFS data), down-ballot political races (governor, mayoral, primary markets show extreme "underconfidence" mispricing), and cross-platform/intra-market arbitrage between Kalshi and Polymarket (one academic paper documented ~$40M of realized arbitrage on Polymarket in a single year). Marquee, liquid markets (major sports sides, Fed decisions, presidential outcomes) are much closer to efficient.
- **For validation, adapt Closing Line Value to prediction markets by benchmarking your entry price against the market's near-resolution price, and treat sample-size math seriously**: for low-frequency events (elections, climate thresholds), detecting a realistic ~2% edge requires on the order of ~350 independent resolved positions at 80% power — meaning a slow-resolving political/climate book may need multiple cycles before you can distinguish skill from luck.

## Key Findings

1. **Favorite-longshot bias is the most replicated inefficiency and is present on Kalshi.** Bürgi, Deng & Whelan (CEPR, Feb 2026; CEPR DP 20631), analyzing 300,000+ Kalshi contracts, found prices are broadly informative and improve toward expiry, but "investors who buy contracts costing less than 10c lose over 60 percent of their money," while "there is statistically significant evidence that contracts with prices above 50c earn a small positive rate of return." Takers lose almost 32% on average versus about 10% for makers, and the average pre-fee return across contracts is −20%.
2. **Polymarket may not share the same aggregate bias — the story is domain- and lifecycle-specific.** A 124-million-trade Polymarket study found no general longshot bias, with prices closely tracking realized probabilities and slightly beating bookmaker odds; the documented distortion is better described as a "Yes Bias" (overpaying for the affirmative), with the most capitalized traders systematically underperforming smaller ones and the most vocal participants adding no informational edge. Consistent with this, blockchain analysis of over 1.7 million Polymarket addresses found roughly 70% have realized losses and only ~30% turned a profit, with fewer than 0.04% of addresses capturing over 70% of total realized profits.
3. **Sports:** Poisson/Skellam scoring models, Elo/rating systems, and regression/ML remain the professional workhorses; the sharpest benchmark is the closing line at low-margin books like Pinnacle, and the Asian handicap soccer market is documented as essentially efficient while traditional 1X2 markets are biased.
4. **Climate/weather:** Kalshi daily temperature markets settle on specific stations; the documented retail edge is that public forecasts (NWS/GFS ensembles) plus real-time METAR observations often beat the market, especially inside 24 hours, because retail anchors on round numbers and underprices forecast certainty.
5. **Down-ballot politics:** Academic forecasting uses hybrid fundamentals + poll-aggregation Bayesian models; the newest microstructure research (Le 2026) shows political markets are the single most mispriced domain on Kalshi, with down-ballot subcategories (governor, mayoral) showing the most extreme "underconfidence."
6. **Kalshi/Polymarket-specific edges are documented and quantified**: intra-market and combinatorial arbitrage on Polymarket (~$40M realized), cross-platform arbitrage (2–5% gaps), and passive liquidity "underwriting" on Kalshi NFL (~$29M aggregate across a season).

## Details

### Domain 1: Sports Betting Markets

**Modeling techniques.** The professional toolkit is well established:
- **Poisson and bivariate/Skellam models** for goal- and point-scoring sports. The canonical lineage is Maher (1982), Dixon & Coles (1997, adding score correlation and a time-decay weighting), and Karlis & Ntzoufras (bivariate Poisson). Each team's expected scoring rate (λ) is estimated from attack/defense strengths and home advantage; the model produces a full scoreline matrix from which any market (moneyline, totals, correct score, BTTS) can be priced. Modern desks blend Poisson with expected-goals (xG) and shot-quality data from providers like Opta and Wyscout to capture chance quality rather than raw goals.
- **Elo and derivative rating systems.** Elo updates a team rating after each game based on result, margin of victory, and location. FiveThirtyEight's CARM-Elo added player-level adjustments (injuries, rest, trades); nfelo extends 538's NFL Elo with QB-specific models plus home-field, rest, and weather adjustments. Pythagorean expectation (points scored/allowed) is a complementary rating. Basic Elo achieved ~64% accuracy on NBA game outcomes in one published study — useful, but limited because it only knows scores, margin, and venue.
- **Regression / ML.** Logistic, probit, and Poisson regression, and increasingly LSTM/transformer models, incorporate recent form, injuries, home advantage, and rest, typically fit via maximum likelihood with L1/L2 regularization.

**Most predictive data/features.** Team ratings (Elo/xG-adjusted), player availability (injuries, rest, lineup leaks), home advantage, rest days, weather (for outdoor sports), and — critically — sharp market prices themselves. Research repeatedly shows bookmaker odds outperform official rankings (e.g., odds beat FIFA rankings at Euro 2008), so a well-built model should decorrelate against, not merely re-derive, the market line.

**Market efficiency.** Sports markets are the most efficient of the three domains, but not uniformly:
- Hegarty & Whelan (2023, *International Journal of Forecasting*; also CEPR) found the **Asian handicap soccer market is efficient** — no favorite-longshot bias, unbiased implied probabilities — while the **traditional home/draw/away market is systematically biased** toward favorites. This is the cleanest illustration that market structure and liquidity, not sport, determine efficiency.
- Closing odds at low-margin books (Pinnacle, ~2.5% margin) are treated as unbiased forecasts of outcome probability and are the standard efficiency benchmark. Several studies (Reading, CBS) find online odds give unbiased, weak-form-efficient forecasts subject to commission.
- Documented soft spots: individuals under-predict the draw ("black-and-white thinking"), some books shade popular teams and react slowly to injury/lineup news, and props/lower-tier markets are softer than main lines.

**Stale-pricing / microstructure signals.** Injury and lineup leaks (positive CLV often clusters 20–40 minutes after leaks), steam moves, opening-line softness before sharp money arrives, and cross-book price gaps between recreational and sharp books. For prediction markets specifically, late informed order flow near resolution (a >5-point move on above-average volume in the final 30–60 minutes) is a documented signal (Ottaviani & Sørensen; Flepp, Nüesch & Franck).

### Domain 2: Climate / Commodities / Weather-Threshold Markets

**How these markets are priced and modeled.** Kalshi runs daily high/low temperature markets across ~20 U.S. cities, each settling on a specific, named station (e.g., Chicago = Midway/KMDW, Dallas = DFW, Houston = Hobby — settlement-station "gotchas" matter), plus precipitation, snowfall, and hurricane markets. The professional pricing approach is explicitly probabilistic:
- **Fit a distribution around a forecast.** The documented retail method: take the NWS/NOAA forecast high, fit a normal distribution around it, apply a small warm-bias correction (one tool uses −1.5°F), and convert to bracket probabilities. NWS 24-hour forecasts land within 3–4°F of the observed high ~80% of the time.
- **Ensemble models.** GFS 31-member ensembles (the same NOAA meteorologists use) and ECMWF/NAM give probability distributions directly (e.g., "27 of 31 members exceed 85°F"), which can be compared to the market's implied probability.
- **Same-day observation edge.** Real-time METAR airport observations reveal the near-certain daily high before the market fully reprices — the highest-confidence, shortest-horizon play.

**Most predictive data.** Free public data is largely sufficient: NWS/NOAA forecasts, GFS/ECMWF/NAM model output, real-time METAR observations, Tropical Tidbits (hurricanes), and Climate Reanalyzer / NOAA Climate.gov (historical anomalies). For commodities: the EIA data calendar, OPEC meetings, storage levels, tanker traffic, refinery runs, and crack spreads for oil/gas; seasonal demand patterns (summer driving, winter heating).

**Market efficiency.** Weather markets are described by multiple practitioner sources as thinner and less competitive than political markets, creating more mispricing for informed traders — resolution is objective (no dispute), events resolve daily (fast compounding), and outcomes are uncorrelated with financial markets. The documented edge: retail anchors on round-number thresholds and underprices forecast certainty, especially inside 24 hours. Beyond day 1, forecast accuracy degrades fast and the edge disappears. Commodities markets on Kalshi (WTI, Brent, gasoline, natural gas, heating oil, gold, silver, copper) settle on futures/benchmark prints; there is no latency game, so the edge is purely in forecasting the level/range better than the crowd — which also means these compete against sophisticated macro traders and are closer to efficient than weather.

**Caveat on the "edge" claims.** Many weather-edge figures (e.g., "85–90% win rate on same-day trades") come from commercial tools selling subscriptions and should be treated as marketing until independently verified. The underlying mechanism (public forecast data beating round-number-anchored retail) is credible and consistent across sources, but after fees and spreads, forecast-market gaps frequently disappear.

### Domain 3: Down-Ballot / Narrow Political & Election Markets

**Modeling techniques (from academic election forecasting).** The state of the art is a **hybrid of fundamentals and poll aggregation in a Bayesian framework**:
- **Fundamentals/structural models** (Hummel & Rothschild; Abramowitz "Time for Change"; Hibbs "bread and peace") use economic indicators, incumbency, past results, and approval to forecast without polls — useful early because they don't need polling data.
- **Poll-aggregation models** synthesize many polls with adjustments for house effects, sampling error, and timing (Linzer 2013; the Economist model of Gelman, Morris, Heidemanns).
- **Hybrid/synthesis models** use the fundamentals forecast as a Bayesian prior for Election Day and update it with polls via a random walk, dynamically shifting weight toward polls as the election nears (the Economist and Decision Desk HQ both do this). For subnational/down-ballot races, models add coattail effects from federal/gubernatorial trends and borrow strength across states hierarchically (see the ScienceDirect subnational-forecasting paper).

**Most predictive data.** Poll averages (weighted by recency, sample size, pollster quality), fundamentals (economy, incumbency, prior margins, generic ballot), and federal/top-of-ticket trends for coattails. Prediction-market prices themselves are a strong long-horizon signal: Berg, Nelson & Rietz (2008, *International Journal of Forecasting* 24(2):283–298), comparing 964 polls over the five presidential elections since 1988, found "the market is closer to the eventual outcome 74% of the time" and "the market significantly outperforms the polls in every election when forecasting more than 100 days in advance."

**Market efficiency — this is where the biggest documented mispricing lives.** The critical, most citable evidence:
- **Le (2026), "Decomposing Crowd Wisdom: Domain-Specific Calibration Dynamics in Prediction Markets" (arXiv 2602.19520).** Using ~353 million trades across ~429,000 Kalshi binary contracts, the paper's four-component decomposition explains 87.3% of in-sample calibration-slope variance (71.5% out-of-sample), with 96.3% posterior predictive coverage. The dominant finding is **persistent underconfidence in political markets — prices are chronically compressed toward 50%, understating the probability of favored outcomes — and it replicates on Polymarket.** Underconfidence is measured by a logistic recalibration slope b > 1. Crucially for a down-ballot system, the effect is **broadly distributed across subcategories**, and the down-ballot ones are among the most extreme: **Governor markets show slopes of 1.19–4.02 and NYC Mayor 1.12–3.18** — wider ranges than Electoral College (1.53–2.87). The bias grows with horizon (long-horizon political slope reaches ~1.74) and with trade size on Kalshi: large trades (>100 contracts) exhibit a calibration slope of 1.74 versus 1.19 for single-contract trades, "Δ=0.53, 95% confidence interval [0.29,0.75]," an effect that "does not replicate on Polymarket (Δ=0.11, [−0.15,0.39])." *(Preprint; not yet peer-reviewed; a v1/v2 data-size discrepancy exists — 292M/327k vs 353M/429k trades/contracts.)*
- **Clinton & Huang (2025), "Prediction Markets? The Accuracy and Efficiency of $2.4 Billion in the 2024 Presidential Election" (SocArXiv d5yx2).** Across "more than 2,500 political prediction markets… during the final five weeks of the 2024 U.S. presidential campaign involving more than two billion dollars," the study found "while 93% of PredictIt markets correctly predicted outcomes better than chance, accuracy fell to 78% on Kalshi and 67% on Polymarket" — the largest-volume platform was least accurate. Even accurate markets showed little efficiency: identical contracts diverged across exchanges, daily price changes were weakly correlated or negatively autocorrelated (58% of Polymarket national presidential markets showed negative serial correlation — spikes reversed the next day), and arbitrage peaked in the final two weeks. Clinton's summary: "Traders weren't reacting to political reality. They were reacting to each other." Kalshi spokesman Jack Such called the methodology one that "completely misunderstands prediction markets, and shouldn't be taken seriously"; the study is presidential-cycle-focused and does not cleanly isolate down-ballot as a variable.
- **PredictIt inefficiency literature.** Studies find PredictIt contracts chronically mispriced (large arbitrage in 2016, smaller in 2020) versus the Iowa Electronic Markets, attributable to PredictIt's profit fees and $850 position caps that prevent arbitrageurs from correcting prices. Singh documents persistent, exploitable anomalies in PredictIt's 2020 markets driven by traders acting on election misinformation.

**Why down-ballot is the sweet spot.** Practitioner consensus (multiple commercial sources) locates edge in thin, low-attention markets: marquee races trade with depth and are efficient; niche/down-ballot/primary markets are thin, with wider effective spreads and slower professional attention. Typical retail edge is quoted at 1–5¢ per contract after fees on the minority of contracts that are actually mispriced, with the thinnest markets occasionally offering 15–40¢ edges. The discipline is skipping fairly priced contracts and acting only on the small fraction that are wrong. *(These edge figures are from commercial/blog-tier sources, not peer-reviewed.)*

### Cross-Cutting: CLV-Equivalent Validation and Sample-Size Requirements

**Closing Line Value adapted to prediction markets.** In sportsbooks, CLV compares the odds you took to the closing line at a sharp book (Pinnacle), because the close is the most efficient, information-complete price; consistently beating it is the strongest predictor of long-term profit — more reliable than win rate. CLV% is typically computed as (closing implied prob − entry implied prob), often on a no-vig basis.

For prediction markets, the natural adaptation is to **benchmark your entry price against the market's near-resolution or settlement price** (the prediction-market analogue of the "close"):
- On Kalshi/Polymarket there is no single "kickoff" close, so use the price in a defined window before resolution (e.g., last-traded VWAP in the final hours, or the price at T-minus-1-day for slow markets) as the efficiency benchmark. Bürgi et al. establish the empirical basis: Kalshi prices "improve in accuracy as markets approach closing," so the near-resolution price is the best available truth proxy.
- The validation logic (per practitioner model-validation writing): positive average CLV over hundreds of positions indicates a genuine edge; it separates process quality from outcome noise before results accrue. Pair it with realized Brier/log-loss scores against outcomes — CLV tests whether you beat the market, calibration tests whether your probabilities are true.
- **Caveat specific to prediction markets:** the "close" is only a valid benchmark where the market is efficient. Le (2026) and Clinton & Huang (2025) show political prediction-market closing prices are biased (underconfident) and near-resolution inaccuracy actually rises in some political markets — so in the very domains with the most edge, CLV must be interpreted cautiously and supplemented with outcome-based calibration over full cycles. Dynamic hedging (Axén & Cortis 2020) is the trading counterpart: if your entry price is displaced from where the market later settles, you can close the position for a locked-in profit regardless of outcome, which is a direct monetization of positive CLV.

**Sample size to distinguish edge from variance.** This is the binding constraint for low-frequency events. The clearest published formula comes from the Foresight Arena benchmark (arXiv 2605.00420): to detect a true edge α* at 5% significance and 80% power, with maximal outcome variance and typical boldness (|b−p|=0.15), required predictions **n ≳ 0.139/(α*)²**. This yields:
- α* = 0.02 (a well-calibrated agent vs. an efficient market): **~348 predictions**
- α* = 0.01 (a frontier model with a small edge): **~1,392 predictions**
- α* = 0.005 (marginal skill): **~5,567 predictions**
- α* = 0.05 (a strong, rare edge): **~56 predictions**

The practical implication: fast-resolving sports and daily weather markets can accumulate the needed sample within a season or a few months, so a 2% edge can be validated relatively quickly. But slow-resolving markets — elections resolving over months, climate thresholds resolving seasonally — will produce only dozens of independent resolutions per cycle, meaning a genuine 2% edge may take **multiple election cycles or several years** to distinguish from luck. This argues for (a) validating models primarily via CLV rather than waiting for outcomes in slow markets, (b) treating dozens of bets as statistically meaningless (multiple practitioner sources stress small samples are "prone to misleading variance"), and (c) prioritizing high-frequency domains for edge validation before deploying capital in low-frequency ones.

### Kalshi/Polymarket-Specific Findings

The richest single practitioner-academic synthesis is Frenzy Capital's review of 20 academic papers ("Trading Strategies for Prediction Markets," Apr 2026), which maps documented inefficiencies to platform-specific tactics. The most relevant, evidence-backed strategies:

1. **Favorite-longshot / "buy No on overpriced longshots."** Because neither Kalshi nor Polymarket allows naked shorting, you express a bearish view by buying the "No" contract. Sweet spots: buy No when Yes is $0.05–0.15; buy Yes directly when it's $0.75–0.92. Expected edge 2–5% per contract; very high confidence.
2. **Intra-market and combinatorial arbitrage on Polymarket** (Saguillo, Ghafouri, Kiffer & Suárez-Tangil 2025, "Unravelling the Probabilistic Forest," arXiv:2508.03474, AFT 2025). Analyzing 86 million bets across 17,218 conditions, the authors report: "We find a realized estimate of 40 million USD of profit extracted across both types of arbitrage during our measurement period" (April 2024–April 2025). Two forms: (a) YES+NO ≠ $1.00 rebalancing, and (b) combinatorial mispricing across logically related NegRisk markets (e.g., "Democratic nominee" vs. "Democrat wins presidency"). Sports had the most frequent opportunities; politics had the largest per-opportunity profits; mispricings can persist for hours. Media reporting (Decrypt/PANews) corroborates the ~$40M figure and notes the phenomenon extends to Kalshi.
3. **Cross-platform arbitrage (Kalshi ↔ Polymarket).** Practitioner tools (DeFi Rate, ArbBets, open-source bots) report 2–5% price gaps on major events and scan for cases where buying YES on one platform and NO on the other sums to <$1.00 after fees. One commercial "2026 arbitrage report" cites a ~9.6% average arb rate across cross-platform scans (commercial source — treat as indicative, not verified). Watch: markets must resolve on the identical underlying event; text-similarity matching produces false positives.
4. **Passive liquidity provision / "underwriting" on Kalshi** (Palumbo 2026, "A Microstructure Perspective on Prediction Markets," SSRN). Kalshi NFL liquidity providers systematically end the season holding directional exposure (they're underwriters, not classical market-makers), with aggregate positive profit ~$29M across an NFL season — but with significant weekly drawdowns, so it requires diversification across many uncorrelated events and disciplined wider quoting when flow is one-sided.
5. **Informed-flow following and long-horizon forecasting edges** (see Domains above): follow sharp late order flow near resolution; and at 60–100+ day horizons, trust market prices over polls and fade poll-driven overreactions.

**Fee mechanics that shape all of the above.** Kalshi uses a quadratic fee (cheapest near 1¢/99¢, most expensive near 50¢), which interacts directly with favorite-longshot trades. Axén & Cortis (2020) show that when fees are charged per-leg on winnings, a hedge/round-trip needs odds to move ~15%+ to profit (break-even scales with 1/(1−fee)²), whereas when fees are on net P&L the fee drops out of the break-even condition — so a system should route dynamic-hedging strategies to net-P&L fee venues.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build and validate on fast-resolving, objective markets first (weeks 1–12).**
- Start with Kalshi daily weather (temperature) markets. Build the NWS-high + normal-distribution + warm-bias-correction model, cross-checked against GFS ensembles and same-day METAR. Trade only inside 24 hours of settlement where the edge is real. This domain gives you the fastest path to a statistically meaningful sample (hundreds of resolutions in a quarter) to validate the whole pipeline.
- In parallel, build a sports probability engine (Poisson/Skellam + xG for soccer; Elo/nfelo-style ratings for NFL/NBA) and benchmark it against Pinnacle closing lines, not just outcomes.
- **Instrument CLV from day one:** log entry price and a defined near-resolution benchmark price for every position; track average CLV as your primary KPI.

**Stage 2 — Add the highest-edge low-frequency domain: down-ballot politics (months 3–9).**
- Build a fundamentals + poll-aggregation Bayesian model for governor, Senate, House, mayoral, and primary races. Focus capital on thin, low-attention markets, which the research and practitioners agree are the most mispriced.
- Exploit the documented "political underconfidence": when your model says a favorite is at 80% but the market compresses it toward 50–65%, buy the favorite. This is the highest-conviction structural edge in the report.
- Because outcomes are sparse, validate via CLV and cross-cycle calibration, not short-run P&L.

**Stage 3 — Layer arbitrage and microstructure modules (ongoing).**
- Add a cross-platform scanner (Kalshi vs. Polymarket) and a Polymarket intra-market/combinatorial arbitrage scanner (YES+NO deviations and NegRisk inconsistencies), with strict same-event verification and full fee/gas/slippage accounting.
- Consider a passive-LP/underwriting module on Kalshi only if you have API connectivity, a capital base that survives correlated drawdowns, and diversification across many events.

**Benchmarks / thresholds that change the plan:**
- If average CLV over 300+ positions in a domain is not clearly positive, do not scale capital there — the model has no demonstrated edge.
- Require an expected edge net of fees above a floor (~1.5–2% on liquid markets; demand more on thin ones to cover spread/slippage).
- Size with fractional Kelly, capped by visible order-book liquidity (never take a position that itself moves the price past your edge).
- Treat any single-domain sample under ~50 resolved bets as uninformative; do not update strategy on it.
- Kill or re-price any arbitrage/LP strategy when realized net edge falls below fees for a sustained window — these edges decay as platforms mature and attract sophisticated flow.

## Caveats

- **Source quality varies sharply.** The strongest claims rest on academic work (Bürgi/Deng/Whelan; Le 2026; Clinton & Huang; Saguillo et al.; Palumbo; the Polymarket 124M-trade study). Many edge figures — weather win rates, "9.6% arb rate," "1–5¢ retail edge," cross-platform gap sizes — come from commercial tools and blogs with an incentive to advertise opportunity; treat these as directional, not verified.
- **Preprint and dispute risk.** Le (2026) is an unreviewed preprint with an internal data-size discrepancy; Kalshi publicly disputes the Clinton & Huang methodology. The core qualitative findings (political underconfidence, thin-market mispricing, arbitrage persistence) are corroborated across multiple independent sources, but specific magnitudes may shift.
- **The favorite-longshot vs. no-longshot-bias conflict is real.** Kalshi shows a clear aggregate favorite-longshot bias (Bürgi et al.); a large Polymarket study finds no general longshot bias and even a "favorite-longshot reversal" in some data; Le finds the classic bias largely dissolves once you control for contract lifecycle and is better described as underconfidence/"Yes bias." Practical implication: bias direction is platform-, domain-, and lifecycle-specific — calibrate per market segment, don't assume a universal rule.
- **Concentration of profits is extreme.** On Polymarket, blockchain analysis found ~70% of over 1.7 million addresses realized losses and fewer than 0.04% of addresses captured over 70% of realized profits — a sober reminder that documented edges accrue to a small, sophisticated minority.
- **Efficiency is a moving target.** Prediction-market volumes are growing fast (Kalshi reported record monthly notional volumes through 2025–2026, e.g., $17.91B in May), which tends to erode edges over time; arbitrage closes fastest, structural biases persist longest.
- **Execution, resolution, regulatory, and platform risks** are material: thin books mean slippage; ambiguous resolution criteria can turn winners into losers; Polymarket's U.S. regulatory status has been contested (FBI raid on CEO in late 2024; CFTC pathway to U.S. operation via Polymarket US in 2025); and platform/withdrawal risk argues for diversifying across venues.
- **This report is informational, not investment advice.** All betting/trading involves substantial risk of loss, and academic/backtested edges are not guarantees of future results.
